The 5 specs that actually matter

Do not shop by the alarm count on the box. What decides whether the kit earns its place on your dash is the sensor behind the alarm, the power behind the display, and the vehicle it has to fit:

  1. Accuracy, not the alarm count. A sensor that reads three PSI off can sound the low-pressure alarm on a tire that is fine, or stay quiet on one that is not — and every false alarm trains you to ignore the real one. The TM7 publishes ±1.5 PSI, the tightest figure on this page; the M7-3 and TM8 publish ±3 PSI. The check that matters is the sample against a known-good gauge. Hold every kit on this page to that test, including ours.
  2. Screw-on sensors: easy on, easy off. All four kits here use external sensors that screw onto the valve stem — no tire removal, no shop visit, a ten-minute install. The trade-off is the one nobody prints on the box: the sensors sit in plain sight and can be stolen, and a tire rotation means re-pairing the corner. Anti-theft nuts and a setup card you keep in the glovebox are the honest answer to both.
  3. Where the display gets its power. A solar display charges itself off the dash and leaves your 12V socket free for the inflator plug that actually fixes the tire. The TM7's display plugs into the cigarette lighter instead — it works fine, but it takes the socket your inflator wants, and one buyer review on the listing says exactly that: a driver who bought a solar version second after finding the 12V-only display limiting. Count the socket before you count the saving.
  4. Which alarms, not how many. Five modes and six modes sound different until you read them: fast leak, high pressure, low pressure, high temperature, sensor low battery. The sixth mode on the TM7 and TM8 is the extra alarm; the M7-3 covers the same five core cases. What matters is the slow leak — the one that drops a tire two PSI a week and strands you on a cold morning — and every kit here claims to catch it. The alarm is only as good as the accuracy behind it.
  5. Vehicle fit, especially length. A sensor's radio has to reach the display, and distance is the quiet limit on this whole category. Tymate does not recommend the M7-3 for RVs or trailers, and the TM8 is meant for vehicles under 20 feet, with no repeater option. For a daily car none of this matters. For a trailer or a long RV it is the whole decision — buy the kit built for the length, or buy the repeater with it.

Before you buy: know what your TPMS has to prove

Your TPMS kit's job is not to show you a number. It is to be right about that number, on a cold morning, on a tire that is losing two PSI a week, before the leak becomes a flat. The real test is a week of driving, not the daytime demo on the product page: the sensor's reading against a known-good gauge, on all four corners, after seven days on the road. Do external TPMS sensors get stolen?

They can be — screw-on sensors sit on the valve stem in plain sight. Anti-theft nuts are the usual answer, and RoadReady plans to include them in the box, along with a one-page setup card. Pairing is the other half of the job: the four sensors come pre-labelled for their corners, and swapping a tire means re-pairing that corner, so keep the setup card with the car.

For a daily car, yes — any car with standard valve stems takes a screw-on sensor kit.
