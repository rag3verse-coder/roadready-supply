Trunk Kit Checklist: 10 Things Every Car Should Carry

Most trunks contain a mystery: a loose jack, a flashlight with dead batteries, and good intentions. This checklist replaces the mystery with ten items, in the order you will actually reach for them — tick each one off, print the page, and leave the printout in the trunk so the next person who borrows the car inherits a stocked car, not a guessing game.

The checklist

1. Portable tire inflator kit — the item that starts the list

A pump rated to 150 PSI with a settable auto-stop, a live pressure readout, adapters for cars, motorcycles, bicycles, and balls, and a case. The non-negotiable detail: two power routes — a built-in rechargeable cell plus a 12V car-socket cable — so a dead battery never makes it a brick. This is the item that handles the most common roadside problem, which is why it is first: a slow leak at night is fixed in ten minutes with this and becomes a tow without it. Run the stranded-cost math if you want the dollar version of that sentence.

2. Tire pressure gauge (a second opinion)

The inflator reads pressure while it works, but a small standalone gauge is the backup that settles arguments — yours against the dashboard light, or yours against a gas-station pump that has been dropped a hundred times. Keep it in the same case as the inflator so the two are never separated.

3. Flashlight with fresh batteries — plus a headlamp if you have one

Half of all roadside work happens in the dark, and a phone flashlight occupies the hand you need for the valve. A headlamp is the upgrade that matters: both hands free, light exactly where you are looking. Check the batteries every season change; dead-battery flashlights are trunk decoration.

4. Reflective vest + warning triangle

Visibility is the safety item people skip because it feels unglamorous, and it is the item that matters most on a fast road at night. A vest costs little, folds flat, and makes you a person instead of a shape in headlights. The triangle goes behind the car, not beside it — distance is what gives oncoming drivers time.

5. Jumper cables or a portable jump pack

A dead battery is the second most common reason a car does not move, and it pairs cruelly with a flat tire: the car that will not start cannot drive to air. A jump pack that holds its own charge is the self-sufficient version; cables are the cheaper version that needs a second car. Either beats waiting for one.

6. First-aid kit, stocked for cuts and headaches

Not a trauma bay — a real kit for real roadside injuries: adhesive bandages in several sizes, gauze, tape, antiseptic wipes, pain relief, and any personal medication. Roadside work involves metal, pressure, and cold hands; small cuts are common and, untreated in a dirty trunk environment, annoying for days.

7. Blanket or emergency mylar blanket

A wait that is comfortable in summer is miserable in winter, and a car with a dead battery has no heat. A blanket turns a two-hour wait from a shivering ordeal into an inconvenience, and a mylar blanket weighs nothing and fits in a glovebox. If you drive with kids or older passengers, this item moves up the list.

8. Water + a snack that does not spoil

Two bottles of water and something shelf-stable — granola bars, trail mix, crackers. Dehydration makes bad decisions worse, and a wait that stretches past a meal turns a flat tire into a hungry, dehydrated flat tire. Rotate the snack when you rotate the seasons; stale is better than absent, but fresh is free.

9. Phone charger that works without the engine running

A cable for the car's socket is not enough when the car is the problem. A small power bank, kept charged, is what keeps the phone — the map, the tow call, the person you text "I'm okay" — alive through a long wait. Check its charge monthly; a dead power bank is a paperweight with a USB port.

10. Your car's own numbers, written down

A card with your tire pressure (door-jamb number, not the sidewall maximum), your roadside assistance number, your insurer's claims line, and one emergency contact. Phones die and memories blank under stress; a card does not. If you have never found your pressure number, the PSI Finder shows you where the sticker lives.

Why this order

The list is ordered by frequency, not by drama. Tire pressure problems are the most common roadside event, so the inflator is first. A dead battery is second. Visibility and light are not "events" at all — they are the conditions under which every other event happens, which is why they sit high despite never being the reason you stopped. The blanket, water, and charger are not repair tools; they are wait tools, and waits are the part of a breakdown that actually hurts.

A trunk kit is not a survival fantasy. It is ten ordinary items that turn the three ordinary breakdowns — flat, dead, dark — into handled problems.

What is deliberately not on the list: a full-size spare (most modern cars do not carry one, and a space-saver or repair kit is a car-specific decision), flares (effective but a fire risk in a dry trunk in summer — the reflective triangle covers the same job), and tools beyond the basics (a lug wrench belongs with the spare if your car has one; otherwise it is weight). Ten items you will actually carry beat twenty you will leave in the garage.

The seasonal re-check: four minutes, four times a year

A kit rots quietly. Batteries drain, snacks expire, power banks empty themselves, and the inflator's charge fades in a cold trunk. Tie the re-check to something you already do four times a year — the first weekend of each season works — and run this pass:

  1. Charge: inflator cell, jump pack, power bank — all three to full.
  2. Replace: flashlight batteries, expired snacks, any water bottle that has spent a summer baking.
  3. Re-read: tire pressures cold, before driving — cold mornings drop pressure roughly 1 PSI per 10°F, so the number you set in July is not the number you need in January.
  4. Re-print: if anything changed — a new phone number, a new car, a new assistance plan — print a fresh card and retire the old one.

Four minutes, four times a year, and the trunk stays a kit instead of becoming a museum.

FAQ

Do I really need all ten items, or just the inflator?

The inflator handles the most common problem, and it is the only item on this list that RoadReady sells. The other nine exist because breakdowns compound: a flat tire at night needs light and visibility, a dead battery needs a jump, and a long wait needs warmth, water, and a charged phone. Start with the inflator; add one item a week and the trunk is complete in two months.

Where should the kit live — trunk, back seat, garage?

In the car, always. A kit in the garage helps the car in the driveway; the car that needs it is thirty miles away. Within the car, the trunk is right for most drivers — but if your trunk requires the car to be running to open, keep the inflator itself in the cabin.

How do I find my tire pressure number for item #10?

On the sticker inside the driver's door jamb — not the max figure molded into the tire itself, which is an upper limit rather than the recommended setting. The PSI Finder explains how to locate that sticker for your kind of vehicle, and the TPMS Decoder explains what to do when the dashboard light comes on.

What about winter-specific items?

In cold climates, add a small shovel, a bag of kitty litter or sand for traction, and a second blanket — and re-read the cold-pressure rule: roughly 1 PSI lost per 10°F drop. The winter trunk guide covers the seasonal version of this list in full.
