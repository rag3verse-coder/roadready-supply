The 5 specs that actually matter

The resolution on the box is the number most likely to mislead you. What decides whether your footage holds up after a crash is everything around it:

  1. Real resolution, not file resolution. A camera can write a 3840×2160 file from a 1080p sensor — that is upscaling, not 4K, and a license plate stays unreadable no matter what the file is called. REDTIGER itself defines its 4K by the exported file size. The check that matters is sample footage on a night drive: can you read a plate in the dark. Judge every camera on this page by that test, including ours.
  2. Both channels at once, or only one. Some cameras only hit their headline resolution with the rear camera disconnected — the GKU D600 line, for example, loses HDR when both channels run. A dual cam that drops quality the moment the rear camera is plugged in is a front-only camera wearing a dual badge. Check that the front and rear record together at the stated resolution.
  3. The sensor, not the megapixels. Night footage is where cheap cameras fail and where a good sensor earns its price. The GKU's Sony STARVIS 2 sensor is the reason its night footage is the one reviewers keep coming back to. A big resolution number on a weak sensor is a daylight camera; a strong sensor on a modest resolution is a camera that works at 2 AM.
  4. Parking mode's real cost. "24-hour parking mode" sounds free until you read the fine print: most budget cameras need a separate hardwire kit to record while the car is off, and that kit is usually sold separately. The Pelsee P3 states it plainly — hardwire kit required. Before you compare prices, add the hardwire kit to any camera that does not include it.
  5. What is in the box. A dash cam without a memory card is a camera that cannot record, and a 64GB card is a $15–$25 purchase on top of the price on the listing. The GKU and the Pelsee both include a 64GB card; the REDTIGER includes 32GB. A $45 camera that needs a $20 card is a $65 camera — count the card before you count the saving.

Is a budget dash cam's 4K actually 4K?

Not always. Cheap 4K is often 1080p footage upscaled to a 4K file, and some brands define 4K by the exported file size rather than the sensor. The check that matters is real sample footage on a night drive: can you read a license plate in the dark. RoadReady will not print 4K on this page until its sample proves it.

Do I need a rear camera, or is front-only enough?

Front-only covers the crash in front of you, which is the most common one. A rear camera covers the rear-end hit, the parking-lot bump and the passenger dispute — the exact cases rideshare and delivery drivers buy a camera for. If you drive for work, dual-channel is worth it; if you only commute, front-only plus a good sensor can be enough.

What does parking mode really cost?

The camera may advertise 24-hour parking mode, but most budget cams need a separate hardwire kit to run it while the car is off, and that kit is usually sold separately. Check the box: if the hardwire kit is not included, add its price to the real cost of the camera before you compare.
