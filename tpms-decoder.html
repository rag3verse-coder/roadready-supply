Your tire light is on. Here's what it means.

That horseshoe-with-an-exclamation-mark light is your TPMS — tire pressure monitoring system. It trips when a tire drops roughly 25% below its recommended pressure. Cold mornings trip it too: pressure falls as temperature falls.

Is it safe to drive?

A short, slow trip to check and fill is fine. Ignoring it is not: under-inflated tires brake longer, handle worse, wear faster, and are more likely to blow out at highway speed. If the tire looks visibly flat or the car pulls hard, put the spare on or call for help instead of driving on it.

Step 1 — Check all four tires

Use a gauge, not your eyes — a tire can be 10 PSI low and still look fine. Check them cold if you can. Write down each reading.

Step 2 — Fill to the door jamb number

Your driver's door jamb sticker lists the right PSI — not the number on the tire sidewall (that is the tire's maximum). Fill each tire to the sticker number. The RoadReady kit's gauge reads live and auto-stops at your PSI.

Step 3 — Drive a few minutes

Most TPMS systems reset on their own after a short drive at correct pressure. If the light stays on with correct pressure, you likely have a slow leak or a failing sensor — get it checked at a shop.

Why it keeps coming back

Three usual suspects: a slow leak (nail, valve stem, bead seal), temperature swings (every 10°F drop costs roughly 1 PSI), or a dying TPMS sensor battery (they last about 5–10 years). A tire that needs air every week has a leak — fill it, then get it patched.
