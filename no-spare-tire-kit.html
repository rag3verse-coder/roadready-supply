Why the spare is gone

Carmakers deleted the spare to save weight and hit fuel-economy targets. A full-size spare, a jack and a wrench weigh real pounds, and every pound costs fuel across a whole model line. So the spare quietly became an option, then a sealant kit, then nothing at all.

The numbers show how far it went. AAA found that 28 percent of 2017 model-year vehicles were sold without a spare as standard equipment, up from 5 percent of 2006 models. An earlier AAA study put it at 36 percent of 2015 models, adding up to about 29 million cars on American roads with no spare. Consumer Reports, counting the cars in its own test fleet since 2020, found only 9 percent with a full-size spare — 46 percent carried a space-saver donut, 34 percent a sealant kit, 7 percent run-flat tires, and 4 percent nothing at all.

The flats did not go away with the spares. AAA said it came to the rescue of more than 450,000 members in one year who had a flat and no spare. That is the gap this page is for: the car changed, and the trunk did not catch up.

What your car actually came with — check it today

Do this in your driveway, in daylight, before you ever need it:

  1. Lift the trunk floor panel. The wheel well underneath is where the spare lives, or lived. A full-size spare or a donut sits there with a jack and lug wrench.
  2. If there is a compressor and a bottle instead, that is a sealant kit. It is what the factory left you in place of a spare. Note the date printed on the sealant bottle — sealant expires, and AAA has reported that factory inflator kits can fail in as little as four years whether they were ever used or not. A kit that has sat untouched since the car was new may not work when you reach for it.
  3. If the well is empty, your car came with nothing. Some cars — many EVs and a growing list of gas models — ship with an empty well and no kit at all. That is the worst case, and also the simplest to fix: the trunk is yours to equip.
  4. Run-flats are the third answer. If your tires are run-flats, the car may have no spare because the tires themselves are the backup — they can be driven a limited distance after losing pressure. Check the sidewall: run-flats are marked, and your owner's manual states the distance and speed limit. It is short, and it is meant to get you to a shop, not to finish the trip.

Whatever you find, put that number and your roadside assistance number on a card in the glove box — the right number is printed on the label inside the driver's door frame; the tire's own sidewall only shows its maximum. If that number means nothing to you yet, the PSI Finder finds your range by vehicle type, and the TPMS Decoder covers the dashboard light — what it means, whether to keep driving, and the three-step fix.

What a kit can and cannot fix — the honest line

An inflator is the right answer for the most common flats, and the wrong answer for the worst ones. Saying which is which matters more than selling the kit:

  • It can fix: a tire that is low, a slow leak, and a nail or screw in the tread. Dial in the door-jamb number, hit start, and let auto-stop hold it there — then drive to a shop under your own power. Most roadside tire trouble is this kind.
  • It cannot fix: a blowout, a shredded tire, or any damage to the sidewall. AAA's own testing found inflator kits cannot provide even a temporary fix for many of those problems. No amount of air helps a tire whose structure is gone — and driving on it destroys the wheel too.
  • It cannot replace a shop visit. A topped-off tire with a nail in it is a tire on its way to a repair, not a repaired tire. Air gets you to the shop; the shop is where the tire actually gets fixed.

That limit is exactly why the plan below starts with looking at the tire before touching the kit. Five seconds of looking decides whether you fill and drive, or call for a tow.

The no-spare roadside plan, in five steps

When the light comes on or the tire goes soft, and there is no spare to change to:

  1. Get somewhere safe and lit. A parking lot or a wide shoulder beats the travel lane every time. Hazards on, engine running if you need the 12V outlet later.
  2. Look at the tire before you fill it. A nail in the tread is a fill-and-drive. A cut in the sidewall, a bulge, or a tire that is visibly shredded is a tow — do not keep adding air to it. If the tire went flat within minutes of topping off, stop and call.
  3. Fill to the door-jamb number. Screw the hose on, set the number, press start. Topping a tire up by a few PSI takes roughly a minute; a tire that started nearly flat takes several. Watch the gauge while it fills — if the number barely moves, the air is escaping faster than the kit can add it, and that is a tow too.
  4. Drive to the nearest shop, slowly and directly. No highway speed on a topped-off tire with a nail in it. The destination is a tire shop, not home — get the repair or the replacement while the tire still holds.
  5. If it will not hold air, call from safety. Expect $75–$150 for the tow itself, and plan on waiting up to two hours for the truck. That is the cost the kit exists to avoid — but when the tire is beyond air, the tow is the right call, and making it from a safe, lit spot is the whole point of the plan.

If the flat happens at night, the same plan applies with one addition: do not work on the dark side of the car. The flat tire at night guide covers the lighting and visibility half of it, and the slow leak guide covers the case where the same tire keeps going soft every few days.

FAQ

How do I know if my car has a spare tire?

Open the trunk and lift the floor panel. A full-size spare or a space-saver donut sits in the wheel well under that panel, usually with a jack and a lug wrench beside it. If you find a small compressor and a bottle of sealant instead, or an empty well, your car has no spare. Check it in your driveway today, not on the shoulder at night.

Can a portable inflator replace a spare tire?

For the most common flats, yes. An inflator re-fills a tire that is low or has a slow leak from a nail in the tread, which covers most roadside tire trouble, and gets you to a shop under your own power. It cannot fix a blowout, a shredded tire, or sidewall damage — AAA's own testing found inflator kits cannot provide even a temporary fix for those. For those, the plan is a tow, not more air.

What did my car come with instead of a spare?

Usually one of three things: a sealant kit (a small compressor plus a bottle of tire sealant), run-flat tires that can be driven a short distance after losing pressure, or a space-saver donut. Consumer Reports counted the setups on the cars it has tested since 2020: 46 percent had a space-saver, 34 percent a sealant kit, 9 percent a full-size spare, 7 percent run-flats, and 4 percent nothing at all.

Does the factory sealant kit expire?

Yes. The sealant in a factory kit has a shelf life, and AAA has reported that factory inflator kits can fail in as little as four years whether they were ever used or not. If your car is more than a few years old and you have never touched the kit under the trunk floor, check its date and assume it may not work. A standalone inflator kit does not depend on sealant, so it does not have that expiry problem.

What should I keep in my trunk if I have no spare?

Start with the inflator kit — auto-stop, digital gauge — then add a headlamp, reflective triangles or a vest, a phone charger, and a glove-box card with your car's door-jamb PSI and your roadside assistance number. That combination covers the flats an inflator can fix and keeps you visible and reachable for the ones it cannot.
